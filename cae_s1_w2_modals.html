Week 2: The Modal System

Week 1 was about passive constructions — the architecture of formal written English. This week adds the layer that sits on top: modal verbs.


At FCE, modals were largely a matter of meaning: must means obligation, might means possibility. At CAE, that's not enough. The exam tests whether you can distinguish between forms that are close in meaning but grammatically distinct — and whether you can produce them precisely under pressure.


The most important shift at C1 is modal perfects. Must have done, should have done, needn't have done — these forms carry exact logical claims about the past. The examiner uses them as traps because students confuse them constantly.


Today's job: map the system clearly. Name every form. Understand what each one actually claims. Then drill the transformations until the logic is automatic.

The Modal System

Deduction (positive)
must + bare infinitive
"She must know the answer — she studied for weeks."
Deduction (negative)
can't / cannot + bare infinitive
"He can't be serious. That's completely unrealistic."
Note: cannot is more formal than can't in written CAE contexts.
Obligation (internal/speaker-imposed)
must + bare infinitive
"You must submit the form before midnight."
Obligation (external)
have to + bare infinitive
"Employees have to wear ID badges at all times."
Advice / mild obligation
should / ought to + bare infinitive
"The report should be more concise." / "Applicants ought to provide two references."
Note: ought to is slightly stronger and more formal.
Permission / possibility
may / might + bare infinitive
"The results may surprise you." / "It might rain later."
Permission / theoretical possibility
can / could + bare infinitive
"Visitors can park in Zone B." / "This approach could work."
Prediction / habit
will / would + bare infinitive
"The committee will announce its decision next week." / "She would often arrive early."

Form: modal + have + past participle.

Past deduction (positive)
must have + pp
"They must have left before we arrived — the door was locked."
Past deduction (negative)
can't have + pp
"She can't have read the report — she got every fact wrong."
Past possibility
could have / might have + pp
"He could have taken the earlier train." / "The package might have been delivered while we were out."
Past criticism / unfulfilled obligation
should have + pp
"You should have told me sooner. Now it's too late."
Formal equivalent (criticism)
ought to have + pp
"The council ought to have consulted residents first."

THE CRITICAL DISTINCTION

needn't have vs didn't need to: These two forms look similar but make different claims. CAE Part 4 tests this distinction regularly.

  • needn't have + past participle → the action WAS carried out, but it was unnecessary in hindsight.
    Example: "You needn't have booked — there were plenty of tables." (The booking happened.)
  • didn't need to + bare infinitive → the action was not required; whether it happened is unspecified.
    Example: "She didn't need to buy flowers because the host had plenty." (Whether she bought them is unknown.)
⚠️ CAE Trap: The two forms above are close in meaning but grammatically and logically distinct. In Part 4, the source sentence will confirm whether the action was completed. That is the deciding factor.

Semi-modals behave like full verbs (conjugated, followed by to + infinitive) but express modal meanings. They are essential at C1 for register flexibility and appear regularly in CAE Part 4 transformations.

be able to
ability (replaces can)
"She has never been able to understand his reasoning."
be supposed to
expectation or rule
"The report was supposed to be submitted last week." (often implies it wasn't met)
be bound to
strong certainty of future outcome
"With that level of preparation, he is bound to succeed."
be likely to
probability (formal)
"Prices are likely to rise following the announcement."
be due to
scheduled/expected event
"The train is due to arrive at 14:30."
be set to
formal/journalistic prediction
"The new policy is set to affect thousands of workers."
have to / have got to
external obligation
"We have to leave now." (have to is preferred in formal writing over have got to)
Register note: In CAE Part 4, transformations often require switching between a modal and a semi-modal. The key word will determine the direction. Always check whether the target sentence needs to / bare infinitive / -ing form.

This panel covers the specific traps the CAE examiner uses with modal verbs in Part 4 transformations.

TRAP 1 — needn't have vs didn't need to (most tested distinction):
The key word NEED can force either form — the source sentence decides which.

Worked example A:
Source: He bought a new charger, but his old one still worked.
Key word: NEEDN'T
Answer: He needn't have bought a new charger.
Why: The source confirms the charger WAS bought. Use needn't have + pp.

Worked example B:
Source: It wasn't necessary for her to attend the full session.
Key word: NEED
Answer: She didn't need to attend the full session.
Why: No evidence she attended. Not necessary, action unspecified → didn't need to + bare infinitive.
TRAP 2 — must vs must have:
The key word MUST in a Part 4 item about a past event requires must have + pp, not must + bare infinitive. Three-word string: must have [pp]. Never drop have.
TRAP 3 — should: advice or criticism?
SHOULD can target present advice ("You should see a doctor") or past criticism ("You should have told me" = you didn't, and it was wrong). The tense of the source sentence decides. Past completed event + failure to act → should have + pp.
TRAP 4 — Word count:
CAE Part 4 = 3–6 words including the key word. Contractions count as two words: shouldn't = should + not = 2 words. Modal + have + pp phrases are 3 words minimum. Count before writing.
