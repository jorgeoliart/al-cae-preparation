Session 3: Reading the Code

Sessions 1 and 2 this week were about production: building and supplying passive forms under controlled conditions. This session is different. The passive constructions are still here — but now they are the examiner's tool, not yours. They are embedded in a long, dense text, and they are being used to hide meaning, create ambiguity, and build traps into the answer options.

CAE Part 5 is a multiple choice reading task. One long text, six questions, four options each. Three of those options will be wrong. The examiner has constructed each wrong option carefully — it will use words from the text, it will feel plausible, and it will be precisely, specifically incorrect.

The single most important skill in Part 5 is not reading speed. It is the discipline to go back to the text and find the exact evidence before committing to an answer. The answer is always in the ink — not in what seems likely, not in what makes a good story, not in general knowledge about the topic. The text. Every time.

Before reading for comprehension today, there is a two-minute task: scan the first two paragraphs and identify every passive construction. Name the form. This is not a comprehension task — it is a grammar awareness task. It trains the eye to see structure before it sees meaning.

Part 5 strategy — open before you begin, or use for review.

WHAT PART 5 TESTS

Part 5 tests detailed reading comprehension at C1 level. Questions may target: specific factual detail, inference (what the writer implies without stating directly), the writer's attitude or opinion, the meaning of a word or phrase in context, how a text is organised or structured, and the writer's overall purpose. The text is typically argumentative or analytical — a long article, essay, or review of approximately 600–700 words.

HOW TO WORK THROUGH IT

  • Step 1 — Read the whole text without stopping. Do not look at the questions first. Get the shape, register, and argument of the text clearly in mind before you engage with any question.
  • Step 2 — Read question 1. Identify exactly what it is asking: a specific detail, an inference, an attitude, a vocabulary item, or a global point.
  • Step 3 — Go back to the relevant part of the text. Find the exact sentence or clause that answers the question. Do not answer from memory — return to the text every time.
  • Step 4 — Read all four options before selecting. Eliminate the ones that are clearly wrong. For the remaining options, identify precisely why each one is wrong before committing to your answer.
  • Step 5 — Check: can you point to a specific phrase in the text that proves your chosen answer is correct? If not, you are not ready to select it.

THE EXAMINER'S TRAPS IN PART 5

  • Trap 1 — The Copycat: a wrong option uses words or phrases taken directly from the text, giving the impression of accuracy. The words match but the meaning does not. Always check that the option says what the text says — not merely that it uses the same words.
  • Trap 2 — The Movie: a wrong option describes something that seems likely or logical given the topic, but is not stated or implied in the text. General knowledge about the subject is irrelevant. Only the text counts.
  • Trap 3 — The Exaggeration: a wrong option takes a qualified claim in the text and removes the qualification, making it absolute. Words like "always", "never", "all", "conclusively", and "definitively" in an option are warning signs.
  • Trap 4 — The Agent Swap: particularly relevant to passive constructions. A wrong option attributes an action or claim to a different agent than the one specified in the text — or introduces an agent where the text deliberately omitted one. Check who did what.
  • Trap 5 — The Tense Shift: a wrong option changes when something happened, or presents an uncertain future event as a confirmed past one, or vice versa. Passive constructions are especially vulnerable to this — "had been done" and "was being done" are not the same claim.

PASSIVE CONSTRUCTIONS IN PART 5 — WHAT TO WATCH FOR

When you see a passive construction in the text, ask:

  • (a) Who is the agent? Is the agent stated (by + noun) or omitted?
  • (b) What tense? The form carries precise information about time and aspect.
  • (c) Is a modal involved? Modal perfect passives (should have been, could have been, might have been) make specific logical claims — unfulfilled obligation, past possibility, past criticism. These are frequently the basis of inference questions.
  • (d) Is the passive impersonal? (It is claimed / it has been argued / it is widely believed) — if so, the writer is attributing the claim to an unspecified source. They are not endorsing it.

The Battle for the Built Environment

The question of what a city owes its past has never been straightforward. In Britain, the postwar decades were characterised by a confidence — some would say arrogance — in the power of new construction to solve old problems. Slums were cleared, Victorian terraces demolished, and their inhabitants rehoused in tower blocks that were celebrated at the time as models of rational planning. It was not until the damage had been done that the architectural and social costs of this approach were widely acknowledged. By then, entire neighbourhoods that might have been preserved had vanished, and the communities that had inhabited them had been dispersed beyond any possibility of reassembly.

The legislative framework designed to prevent such losses was introduced piecemeal over several decades. Listed building status, first established in the 1940s, was intended to protect structures of special architectural or historic interest from demolition or unsympathetic alteration. Conservation areas followed in 1967, offering a broader form of protection to groups of buildings whose collective character was considered worth preserving. Yet the system has always been criticised for being reactive rather than preventive. Buildings are listed after their significance has been recognised — often only after a demolition threat has been identified. By the time statutory protection is granted, irreversible damage may already have been inflicted.

Critics of the current framework argue that local authorities have been given insufficient powers to act decisively. When a developer applies for permission to demolish a listed building, the burden of proof rests with the planning authority to demonstrate why demolition should be refused — not with the applicant to justify why it should be permitted. This imbalance has been exploited repeatedly, and conservationists have long argued that it should have been corrected at the point when the legislation was last substantially revised, in the 1990s. The opportunity was not taken.

The counterargument, made with equal conviction by developers and some economists, is that conservation legislation has itself become an obstacle to necessary change. Historic designations, they contend, are too frequently applied to buildings of marginal significance, effectively sterilising land that could be used to address acute housing shortages. The designation process, it is claimed, is sometimes driven less by genuine heritage value than by local resistance to development — a form of nimbyism dressed in architectural language. These arguments have found a receptive audience in certain parts of government, and proposals to streamline the designation process have been floated at regular intervals.

What both sides tend to underestimate is the economic case for conservation. Research conducted over the past two decades has consistently shown that well-maintained historic environments attract tourism, stimulate local economies, and command property premiums that generate substantial tax revenues. Cities that invested in their built heritage during the 1990s and 2000s — Glasgow, Liverpool, and Manchester among them — have seen returns that were not anticipated by those who originally resisted the expenditure. The argument that conservation and development are inherently opposed has been challenged by this evidence, though it has not been definitively settled.

What seems clear is that the current framework, designed for a different era of development pressure, is no longer adequate. The question is not whether reform is needed but what form it should take. Conservationists argue for stronger presumptions in favour of retention and greater penalties for deliberate neglect — a tactic sometimes used by developers to make a building's condition deteriorate to the point where demolition becomes unavoidable. Developers counter that the planning system needs to be made more predictable and less susceptible to last-minute objections. Both positions contain legitimate concerns. Whether a political consensus capable of addressing them will be reached remains, for now, an open question.
