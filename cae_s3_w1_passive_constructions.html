Session 3: Words & Movement

In Sessions 1 and 2, we looked at the passive system from two angles: structural manipulation (Part 4) and recognition in running text (Part 2).

Today, we add a third angle: the morphological one. The words that surround passive constructions in formal writing — the nouns and adjectives derived from verbs — are what make academic register possible. Without them, the passive sounds clunky. With them, it sounds controlled.

This session also introduces our first phrasal verb block. This will be a standing feature of every Session 3 from now on. Today's semantic field is movement and direction — six verbs that will end in a production task that is more demanding than it looks.

The most productive suffixes at CAE level:

  • -tion / -sion construct → construction, restore → restoration, divide → division
  • -ment develop → development, employ → employment
  • -ance / -ence perform → performance, refer → reference
  • -al arrive → arrival, refuse → refusal, approve → approval
  • -ure fail → failure, expose → exposure
  • -y discover → discovery, deliver → delivery
Note: The suffix cannot always be predicted from the verb. When two suffixes seem possible (e.g. -ment vs -tion), check which one produces a real word. Both refusal and refusement feel possible — but only refusal actually exists.
  • -ive construct → constructive, innovate → innovative, protect → protective
  • -able / -ible achieve → achievable, rely → reliable, access → accessible
  • -ed / -ing damage → damaged (the damaged building), convince → convincing (a convincing argument)
Note: -able and -ible are not interchangeable — the choice depends on the root. Verbs ending in -ate usually take -able (innovate → innovative is an exception, taking -ive). When unsure, eliminate: ask yourself which form you have actually seen in real texts.

At CAE, the trap is often a negative prefix layered on top of a derivation:

  • un- unreliable, unprotected, underdeveloped
  • in-/im-/ir-/il- inadequate, impractical, irreversible, illegible

The prefix choice depends on the first letter of the base: im- before b/m/p, ir- before r, il- before l, in- elsewhere.

Important: The prefix attaches to the DERIVED form, not the base verb. You must derive the word first, then add the prefix. Example: EMPLOY → unemploy (wrong) → unemployment (correct).

Phrasal Verbs — Week 1: Movement & Direction

Six verbs. Read the example, work out the meaning, then click to confirm.

set off

The delegation set off for Brussels early on Monday morning.

Meaning: To begin a journey or depart for a destination.

"The protesters' march set off a chain of events that would last for months."

break away

A group of senior MPs broke away from the party following the leadership crisis.

Meaning: To separate from a larger group, organisation, or system — often implying disagreement or independence.

"She broke away from the crowd and headed towards the exit."

get through

After three attempts, she finally got through to the minister's office.

Meaning: (1) To successfully make contact with someone. (2) To survive or complete something difficult.

"I don't know how he got through the first year of the programme — it was relentless."

move on

The committee agreed it was time to move on and consider the next item on the agenda.

Meaning: To progress past something — a topic, a situation, a place, or an experience.

"It took her years to move on after the company collapsed."

pull out

The investment firm pulled out of the project citing regulatory concerns.

Meaning: To withdraw from a commitment, agreement, plan, or location.

"The troops began to pull out of the region after the ceasefire was declared."

head off

The prime minister headed off the criticism by announcing an independent inquiry.

Meaning: (1) To leave or set out in a particular direction. (2) To intercept or prevent something from developing.

"We decided to head off before the traffic got too bad."

Production Task

Use all six phrasal verbs from this session to tell the following story. You can speak or write. The verbs can appear in any order. The story must make sense as a coherent narrative — dropping verbs in mechanically won't work.

"A political party is falling apart. Its leader has just announced a controversial decision. Some members are unhappy. A journalist is trying to reach someone for comment. The party conference is tomorrow."
set off break away get through move on pull out head off

The test is not whether you used all six verbs. The test is whether the story is coherent and the verbs are semantically accurate — meaning the right thing in the right context.
