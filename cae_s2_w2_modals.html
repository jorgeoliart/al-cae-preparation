Session 2: Removing the Scaffolding

Session 1 built the system: names, forms, and distinctions. Session 2 tests something different — whether you can read a sentence, sense what's missing, and produce the exact form with no options, no scaffolding, and no key word to prompt you.

This is Part 2: open cloze. One word per gap. The examiner has removed it deliberately. The job is to figure out what it is and write it — nothing more.

The traps here are not about vocabulary. They are about assumption. The biggest mistake students make in Part 2 is deciding what they expect the gap to be before they have read the full sentence. That is The Movie in your head. The answer is in The Ink.

Not every gap in these texts is a modal. Some are not. That is deliberate. The examiner does the same thing in the real exam. Read the whole sentence first, every time.

References — open if needed, or use for review after the exercise.

WHAT PART 2 TESTS

Part 2 tests grammatical awareness at the word level: auxiliary verbs, modal verbs, prepositions, pronouns, conjunctions, relative pronouns, determiners, and discourse markers. It does not test vocabulary. The missing word is almost always a function word or a grammatical form.

HOW TO WORK THROUGH IT

  • Step 1 — Read the whole text first. Do not stop at gaps. Get the meaning and register of the full passage before writing anything.
  • Step 2 — Return to gap 1. Read the full sentence containing the gap — not just the words on either side.
  • Step 3 — Identify the grammatical slot: what part of speech is missing? What structure surrounds the gap?
  • Step 4 — Write one word only. No two-word answers are accepted in Part 2 — except contracted forms, which count as one word (e.g. couldn't). Note: semi-modals like "be able to" span multiple words — in Part 2 these are typically gapped one word at a time within a pre-given frame, not as a full phrase.
  • Step 5 — Check: does your word fit the grammar of the whole sentence, not just the gap?

WHAT THE EXAMINER REMOVES

The examiner tends to remove: auxiliary verbs in complex tenses (have, be, been), modal verbs (must, might, ought, should, need), prepositions after fixed expressions, relative pronouns (which, whose, whom), conjunctions and discourse markers (although, despite, however), and determiners (such, both, either).

COMMON ERRORS

  • Writing two words when only one is allowed
  • Choosing a word that fits the immediate gap but breaks the grammar of the full sentence
  • Assuming every gap in a modal-themed exercise is a modal — some are not

CORE MODALS (one word — gap-eligible as single words):
must / can / could / may / might / will / would / should / ought / need / shall

Modal meanings at a glance:

  • must → strong deduction (positive) or strong obligation
  • can't → strong deduction (negative) — note: in a gap, this is two words; as "cannot" it is one word
  • might / may → possibility (might = lower certainty)
  • should → advice, recommendation, or past criticism (with have)
  • ought → formal equivalent of should (followed by to)
  • need → negated necessity (need not / needn't); in Part 2 typically gapped as "need" within a fixed frame
  • will → confident prediction or habitual action
  • would → past habit, politeness, or conditional

MODAL PERFECTS — the auxiliary "have" is frequently the gapped word:

  • must have [pp] → past deduction (positive)
  • can't have [pp] → past deduction (negative) — "have" is the gappable element
  • might have [pp] → past possibility
  • should have [pp] → past criticism / unfulfilled obligation
  • ought to have [pp] → formal equivalent of should have
  • needn't have [pp] → unnecessary completed action
Note: In a Part 2 gap testing a modal perfect, the gapped word is most commonly "have" — the modal itself is already given in the surrounding text. Recognising this pattern is a key Part 2 skill.

Semi-modals appear in Part 2 when one word of the multi-word form is removed. The surrounding text provides the rest. Common patterns:

  • be able to → gapped as "able" (within "is/was ___ to")
  • be supposed to → gapped as "supposed" (within "is/was ___ to")
  • be bound to → gapped as "bound" (within "is ___ to")
  • be likely to → gapped as "likely" (within "is ___ to")
  • be due to → gapped as "due" (within "is ___ to")
  • be set to → gapped as "set" (within "is ___ to")
Note: The verb "be" and the particle "to" are given — only the adjective/participle is gapped. This is the standard Part 2 treatment of semi-modals.

To finish — answer these out loud

  • In Text 1, gap 6 and in Text 2, gaps 14 and 16 were not modals. Did you notice before or after you checked? What does that tell you about how you were reading?
  • Several gaps were the word "have" inside a modal perfect frame — the modal itself was already in the text. How do you recognise that pattern quickly?

These are not trick questions. There are no wrong answers — the point is to hear yourself explain the system.
