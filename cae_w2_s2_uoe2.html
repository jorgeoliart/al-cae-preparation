Session 2: Word Formation & Morphology

Part 3 at FCE was largely mechanical: add a suffix, check the word class, move on. At CAE, the traps are more subtle. Two suffixes can both be real words but only one is correct in context. Negative prefixes don't follow a single rule. Spelling changes are penalised. Reaching for what sounds right is exactly what the examiner is banking on.


The system is learnable — but only if you slow down and apply it deliberately. The accordion below maps the system. The exercise tests whether you can use it under pressure.

⚠️ Trap: At CAE, the trap is often two plausible nouns from the same base: celebration vs celebratory — one is a noun, one is an adjective. The gap tells you which class you need. Read the sentence structure, not just the base word.

There is no single negative prefix. The choice depends on the base word — and in many cases it simply has to be learned. The patterns below cover the most predictable cases.

⚠️ Trap: The most common CAE trap: un- feels like the default negative prefix — but insignificant, inaccessible, and irreplaceable all reject it. When in doubt, ask: is this base word Latin-derived? If yes, lean toward in- and its variants.

Correct word class, wrong spelling = wrong answer. These are the patterns that produce the most avoidable errors.

  • -tion vs -sion: verbs ending in -t → usually -tion (celebrate → celebration); verbs ending in -d or -de → usually -sion (expand → expansion, divide → division)
  • -ance vs -ence: no reliable rule — must be learned per word. High-frequency CAE pairs: significance (not significence), appearance, existence, independence
  • Consonant doubling: single vowel + single consonant before a suffix starting with a vowel → double the consonant (travel → traveller, forget → unforgettable)
  • Adjectives ending -ic: adverb adds -ally, not -ly (dramatic → dramatically, historic → historically)
  • Silent -e drop: before vowel suffix (explore → exploration, not explorement)
⚠️ Note: One mark per item. A correctly derived word with a spelling error gets zero. Proofread the derived form as a separate step — don't just write it and move on.

Phrasal Verbs — Change & Development

This week's semantic field is change and development. These verbs describe how things evolve, disappear, or transform over time — exactly the kind of language that appears in formal writing about cultural and social change.

turn into
to change and become something different
"Over the centuries, the original ritual gradually turned into a citywide celebration involving thousands of participants."
give way to
to be replaced by something else, especially gradually
"Traditional oral methods of preserving cultural memory have slowly given way to digital archiving."
die out
to disappear completely, usually over a long period
"Without active community support, many regional dialects are at risk of dying out within a generation."
take over
to become dominant or to assume control of something
"Commercial interests have taken over many festivals that were once entirely community-led."
grow out of
to develop from something as its origin or cause
"The modern carnival grew out of a centuries-old harvest festival observed by the region's earliest inhabitants."
phase out
to eliminate or discontinue something gradually and deliberately
"Several governments have moved to phase out subsidies for cultural events that attract primarily international tourists."

The Scenario — use all six

Read the situation below. Write a short paragraph (5–6 sentences) using all six phrasal verbs. The paragraph must be coherent — not a list of disconnected sentences.
