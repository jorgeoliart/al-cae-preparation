Session 3: Stance & Matching

This week's first two sessions were about producing modal forms. Today is about distinguishing between four writers who use the same modal language to say very different things.

Part 7 is multiple matching. The core trap isn't misreading a single text—it's cross-text confusion. Two writers might hold similar positions, but the difference is in the degree of certainty or the exact logical claim. Modal verbs are the signals that determine which writer matches the statement.

This isn't close reading; it's disciplined scanning. Identify the precise claim in the statement, then find the "Receipt" in the ink. No general guesses allowed.

References — open before you begin, or use for review.

HOW TO WORK THROUGH IT

  • Step 1 — Read all four texts quickly to build a mental map of each writer's stance.
  • Step 2 — Read the statement and identify its logical direction (Positive? Conditional? Critical?).
  • Step 3 — Test the statement against the texts. Don't look for topic matches; look for claim matches.
  • Step 4 — Watch for the Modal Swap: "could eventually" is not the same as "will inevitably".

Modal verbs are signals of an intellectual position. Note the difference in force:

  • will / is bound to → Confident assertion / Inevitability.
  • might / could / may → Hedging / Possibility.
  • must / ought to → Strong necessity / Moral obligation.
  • should have / ought to have → Retrospective criticism (Past regret).
Note: A statement about a past failure can only match a writer using a modal perfect (should have/ought to have).

Part 7: Multiple Matching

You are going to read four commentaries about artificial intelligence and creative work. For questions 1–10, choose from the commentaries (A–D). The commentaries may be chosen more than once.

Before you answer: read all four texts through. For each writer, note their overall position and the modal verbs they use most. One minute per text.
Writer A
Those who frame artificial intelligence as an existential threat to human creativity may be overstating the case considerably. Throughout history, new tools have consistently expanded what artists are capable of producing — the printing press, the camera, the synthesiser — and there is no compelling reason to assume that AI will prove categorically different. What AI tools might do, in practice, is liberate artists from the more mechanical aspects of their craft, allowing them to focus on the decisions that genuinely require human judgement and sensibility. A photographer who uses editing software to realise a precise visual effect is not considered less of an artist for doing so; a musician who uses a digital audio workstation is not accused of inauthenticity. The same logic could reasonably be extended to AI. This need not be a story of replacement. It could equally be a story of augmentation — one in which human creativity is extended rather than diminished by the tools available to it.
Writer B
The claim that artificial intelligence is capable of genuine creativity rests on a fundamental confusion between process and experience. What AI systems do — however impressively — is identify statistical patterns in vast quantities of existing material and recombine them in ways that resemble original production. This is not creativity. Creativity, properly understood, requires intentionality, subjective experience, and the capacity to mean something by what one makes. A system that has never felt loss cannot write meaningfully about grief; a system that has never perceived beauty cannot make genuinely aesthetic choices. The outputs of AI tools can be technically accomplished, commercially successful, and widely admired — none of this is in dispute. What they cannot be, by definition, is the product of a creative mind, because the systems that generate them do not have minds. Calling this creativity does a disservice to both the word and the human experience it describes.
Writer C
Whether artificial intelligence is "truly" creative is a question for philosophers, and it is not one that the market has shown much interest in waiting for them to answer. Consumers of creative content — music, visual art, narrative, design — are responding to outputs, not to the processes that generated them. An image that moves people moves them regardless of whether it was produced by a human hand or an algorithm. This is not a moral position; it is an observation about how creative economies actually function. What the current debate consistently underestimates is the scale of the economic transformation already underway. AI-generated content is set to reshape the production, distribution, and pricing of creative work within the decade, and the industries that fail to adapt to this reality should not expect the market to wait for them. The question worth asking is not whether AI can create — it is what this changes, and for whom.
Writer D
The creative industries are facing a structural challenge that ought to have been anticipated — and, in some quarters, was — long before AI tools became widely accessible to the general public. The failure to lobby effectively for protective legislation in the years when that window was still open is one that the sector will be paying for over a considerable period. Human artists are now competing in a market where the marginal cost of producing certain categories of creative content has collapsed almost to zero. This is not a technological inevitability that must simply be accepted; it is a policy failure that can and must be corrected. Regulators who have so far treated AI-generated content as a peripheral concern are bound to find themselves confronted with the consequences of that complacency sooner than they expect. Meaningful protection for human creative labour is not a luxury — it is an obligation that democratic governments have conspicuously failed to meet.
