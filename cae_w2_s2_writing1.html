Session 4: The Report Form

David, this week you've been focused on producing language — mastering transformations, navigating word formation, and building out phrasal verbs. Now, the job shifts. You need to organise that language into a highly specific form that the CAE examiner expects.

A report is not an essay. An essay builds an argument and flows smoothly from start to finish. A report does not. A report delivers precise information in designated sections. It has explicit headings. It is impersonal. It is written for a specific reader with a specific purpose. The reader doesn't want to be charmed by your writing; they want to be informed efficiently.

Right now, your IB training is a liability here. Those 500-word discursive essays you are used to writing are the exact wrong model. The CAE report is tight (220–250 words), strictly sectioned, and deliberately functional. The examiner will actively penalise narrative drift, personal register ("I think..."), and missing structure.

Today, we will analyse a model report, map its non-negotiable framework, and then you will move to the Google Doc to write a single, precise section.

Model Analysis

Task: You have recently attended Up Helly Aa, a fire festival held annually in Shetland, Scotland. Your college has asked you to write a report for other students who may be interested in attending international festivals. Your report should describe the festival, explain its cultural significance, and make recommendations for potential visitors.
Up Helly Aa: A Festival Worth Travelling For

Introduction

Up Helly Aa is an annual fire festival held each January in Lerwick, the capital of the Shetland Islands in northern Scotland. It is widely regarded as one of the most visually dramatic festivals in Europe and draws visitors from across the world. This report describes the festival's key features, outlines its cultural importance, and offers practical recommendations for those considering attending.

The Festival

The centrepiece of Up Helly Aa is a torchlit procession in which over a thousand participants, known as Guizers, carry burning torches through the town before setting fire to a replica Viking longship. The event commemorates Shetland's Norse heritage and has been celebrated in its current form since the late nineteenth century. Attendance is free, though visitors are advised to arrive early to secure a good vantage point along the procession route.

Cultural Significance

The festival serves as a powerful expression of local identity. It might be argued that its significance lies not only in its spectacular imagery but in its function as a community ritual — one in which virtually the entire local population participates in some capacity. This collective dimension could be considered its most distinctive feature.

Recommendations

Visitors would be well advised to book accommodation several months in advance, as availability in Lerwick is extremely limited during the festival period. It is also strongly recommended that appropriate cold-weather clothing be worn, given the January conditions. Those wishing to engage more deeply with the cultural context may find it worthwhile to visit the Shetland Museum prior to attending.

Click each card to deconstruct the report's mechanics:
Four sections: Introduction, The Festival, Cultural Significance, Recommendations. Each heading signals the exact function of that section — the reader knows before reading what kind of information they will find. This is non-negotiable in a CAE report: unnamed or vague headings are penalised.
"It is widely regarded as..." / "Attendance is free, though visitors are advised..." / "It is also strongly recommended that...". The report uses impersonal passive constructions and third-person reference throughout. First-person narration ("I thought the festival was amazing") is a serious register error in a formal report.
"It might be argued that..." / "could be considered its most distinctive feature". These hedging modals signal that the writer is offering an interpretation, not a verified fact. In formal analytical writing, claims about significance or meaning are hedged — presenting them as certainties would be over-assertive and register-inappropriate.
"Visitors would be well advised to..." / "It is also strongly recommended that... be worn" (note the subjunctive) / "may find it worthwhile to...". These are the C1 structures for recommendations in formal writing. "You should bring warm clothes" is B2. "Appropriate cold-weather clothing is strongly recommended" is C1.
Introduction: ~55 words. The Festival: ~75 words. Cultural Significance: ~60 words. Recommendations: ~65 words. Total: ~255 words. Every sentence earns its place — there is no narrative filler, no personal anecdote, no repetition. Each section delivers its information and stops. This is the discipline a test taker needs to build.
It would likely start with a rhetorical hook ("Have you ever witnessed something truly unforgettable?"), use first-person throughout, lack headings, exceed 350 words, and include personal reactions rather than structured information. Every one of those features would cost marks. The CAE report is a different genre — not a harder essay, a different form entirely.

The CAE Report: Structure at a Glance

Introduction

40–50 words
  • State what the report is about and its purpose
  • Name the festival, location, and occasion
  • Signal the sections to follow (optional but clean)
  • Register: impersonal passive preferred — "This report aims to..." / "The following report examines..."

The Festival / Overview

60–70 words
  • Factual description: what happens, when, where, who participates
  • One striking specific detail that makes it memorable
  • No personal reactions — describe, don't narrate

Significance / Analysis

50–60 words
  • Why it matters culturally, historically, or socially
  • Use modal hedging for interpretive claims
  • This is where C1 register earns marks: nominalisations, hedged assertions, formal connectors

Recommendations

50–60 words
  • Practical advice for potential visitors
  • Use formal recommendation structures: "would be well advised to", "it is recommended that", "visitors may wish to"
  • Minimum two concrete, specific recommendations — not vague ("enjoy the festival")
Grammar in Action — Week 2 target: modal hedging

Every report has a Significance section and a Recommendations section. Both demand modal constructions. This is not optional decoration — it is the register marker that separates B2 from C1. In the Google Doc, your Recommendations section must contain at least two distinct modal structures.
