Session 3: Recognition & Precision

Sessions 1 and 2 this week were about production: transforming structures and supplying missing words. Session 3 is about recognition under pressure — reading a sentence with four plausible options and choosing the one that is actually correct.

This is Part 1: multiple choice cloze. Four options per gap. Three of them will look perfectly reasonable. That is the examiner's job. The difference between C1 and lower levels is not whether the answer feels right — it is whether you know precisely why one option is correct and the others are not.

Modal verbs are especially dangerous in Part 1 because the distractors are often near-synonyms. The examiner picks four words that are all roughly in the right semantic area — and then tests whether you know the collocation, the register, or the fixed expression that forces one specific answer.

Today has three parts: the main Part 1 exercise on modal collocations, a shorter MCQ block on phrasal verb meaning distinctions, and the Week 2 phrasal verb micro-strand on change and development.

References — open if needed, or use for review after the exercises.

WHAT PART 1 TESTS

Part 1 tests lexical and lexico-grammatical knowledge: collocations, fixed expressions, phrasal verbs, idioms, and the precise meaning distinctions between near-synonyms. It does not test obscure vocabulary — it tests whether the student knows how words behave in combination with other words.

HOW TO WORK THROUGH IT

  • Step 1 — Read the whole text first, ignoring the gaps. Get the meaning and register before choosing anything.
  • Step 2 — Return to gap 1. Read the full sentence — not just the words on either side of the gap.
  • Step 3 — Before looking at the options: decide what kind of word is missing (modal? adjective? verb? fixed expression particle?). Form a hypothesis.
  • Step 4 — Read all four options. Eliminate the ones that are grammatically or collocationally impossible. Do not stop at the first option that seems right.
  • Step 5 — Check the surviving option(s) against the full sentence, not just the gap. Fixed expressions and collocations must work across the whole clause, not just locally.

THE EXAMINER'S TRAPS IN PART 1

Trap 1 — Near-synonyms: all four options have similar meanings but different collocations. The answer depends on which word combines with the surrounding text, not which word is closest in meaning.
Trap 2 — False friends between options: the same word can appear in different fixed expressions with completely different meanings. Read the surrounding text every time.
Trap 3 — Register: one option is semantically correct but too informal for the text's register, or vice versa. At C1, register precision matters.
Trap 4 — Familiar words in unfamiliar patterns: a word the student knows in one context may behave differently in a fixed expression. Trust the expression, not the feeling.

CERTAINTY AND PROBABILITY

  • be bound to + infinitive → near-certain future outcome (positive)
    "The changes are bound to affect productivity."
  • be likely to + infinitive → probable future outcome
    "She is likely to be appointed next month."
  • be set to + infinitive → formal/journalistic certainty (especially written)
    "The new law is set to come into force in January."
  • be due to + infinitive → scheduled/expected event
    "The report is due to be published next week."

TENDENCY AND BEHAVIOUR

  • tend to + infinitive → general habitual behaviour
    "Students tend to underestimate the difficulty of Part 1."
  • be liable to + infinitive → tendency toward (often negative) outcomes
    "Overconfidence is liable to cause unnecessary errors."
  • be apt to + infinitive → natural tendency (more formal/literary)
    "He is apt to rush his answers under pressure."

EXPECTATION AND OBLIGATION

  • be supposed to + infinitive → expectation set by arrangement or rules
    "The results are supposed to be released today."
  • be meant to + infinitive → intended purpose or expected behaviour
    "This feature is meant to simplify the process."
  • be required to + infinitive → formal/legal obligation
    "All candidates are required to present valid ID."

REPORTING AND BELIEF

  • be said to + infinitive → impersonal reporting (hearsay/general belief)
    "The method is said to be highly effective."
  • be known to + infinitive → established fact attributed to general knowledge
    "She is known to have worked on the original design."
  • be thought to + infinitive → belief attributed to general opinion
    "The practice is thought to date back centuries."
  • be considered to + infinitive → attributed judgement
    "The decision is considered to have been premature."

NECESSITY AND PERMISSION

  • need not + infinitive → absence of necessity (formal register)
    "Candidates need not provide additional documentation."
  • be permitted to + infinitive → formal permission
    "Visitors are not permitted to photograph the exhibits."
Note: In Part 1, the examiner frequently tests distinctions within a single cluster — e.g. four options all from the CERTAINTY cluster (bound / likely / set / due). The answer depends on the precise meaning and register of the full sentence.

Block C — Phrasal verbs: change and development
Week 2 micro-strand · 15 minutes
turn into
to become something different through a gradual or complete process of change
"Over the years, the quiet village turned into a busy tourist destination."
grow out of
to stop doing/needing something as you mature; to originate from something
"His love of adventure grew out of years of travelling with his family."
phase out
to gradually stop using, producing, or doing something over a period of time
"The airline has announced it will phase out its older aircraft by 2027."
phase in
to gradually introduce something over a period of time
"The new grading system will be phased in across all year groups."
die out
to disappear completely, especially over time — used of species, traditions, languages
"Without active preservation efforts, these dialects may die out within decades."
take over
to assume control or responsibility for something, often replacing what came before
"A larger competitor took over the business after the founder retired."
